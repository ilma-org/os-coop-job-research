# Typical projects

## Summary

Google's SRE books describe project work in four groups: building internal tools and automation, capacity planning, onboarding services through Production Readiness Reviews (PRRs), and launch support (01-36, 01-39, 01-42, 01-47). The books give named examples, such as Auxon for capacity planning, datacenter line-card repair automation and the decommissioning of filer-backed home directories (01-39, 01-51, 01-52). They also say long-term project work balances on-call and interrupt work and is needed so team size does not grow with service growth (01-38, 01-40). All examples are from Google's own books. They show the kinds of projects that exist, not a list of what a COOP student would be assigned. Not found yet: a source on intern or student SRE projects.

## Key points

- Internal tools: SREs develop tools such as binary rollout mechanisms, monitoring, or a development environment built on dynamic server composition, and the book says these are full-fledged software engineering projects, not one-off solutions or quick hacks. (01-36, 01-37)
- Why project work exists: achieving linear team growth against exponential service growth needs perpetual automation work and streamlining of tools and processes; long-term project work balances interrupts and on-call work; automation is a force multiplier, not a panacea. (01-38, 01-40, 01-50)
- Capacity planning: Auxon is a tool developed within SRE to automate capacity planning; many Google teams have moved to Intent-based Capacity Planning, which programmatically encodes dependencies and parameters. (01-39, 01-54, 01-56)
- Onboarding services: SRE needs a process, called onboarding, to evaluate a service and institute SRE support. The most typical first step is the Production Readiness Review, a prerequisite for taking responsibility for a service's production aspects. In its analysis phase reviewers look for production shortcomings, and the resulting improvements are prioritized by importance for service reliability. (01-41, 01-42, 01-43, 01-44, 01-45)
- Engagement beyond onboarding: the SRE book proposes giving product development a platform of SRE-validated infrastructure, and the Workbook says an SRE team might engage with a service only after it has been generally available for months or years. (01-46, 01-53)
- Launches: Google defines a launch as any new code that introduces an externally visible change to an application; in the NORAD Tracks Santa example the launch work fell to a special SRE team, the Launch Coordination Engineers. (01-47, 01-109)
- Toil-reduction case studies (Workbook): automation of datacenter line-card repair was the first stage of network repair automation, and moving away from filer-backed home directories was a multiyear effort with multiple internal projects. (01-51, 01-52)

## Sources and limits

Used SRE book chapters 7, 18, 27 and 32 and Workbook chapters 6 and 18, all from https://sre.google/books/. Several claims are examples from case studies, so they show kinds of projects, not what every SRE does. The SRE book footer says copyright 2017 and the Workbook footer says copyright 2018, so each claim states what the book says, not necessarily current practice. Quotes are kept short; open the source for the full passage.
