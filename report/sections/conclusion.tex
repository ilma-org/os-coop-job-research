\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Figure~\ref{fig:chain}, on page~\pageref{fig:chain}, shows how our findings fit together, from the job position to the knowledge it requires.

\paragraph{What we found.}
At Google, SRE means software engineers who run services and build systems to replace manual operations work, with operational work, or toil, capped at 50\% of an SRE's time \cite{sre-book,workbook}. According to the books, the Linux kernel underpins much of Google's production infrastructure \cite{bsrs}, and Borg, the cluster operating system, runs huge numbers of Linux containers \cite{sre-book,workbook}. It runs in Google-designed datacenters where, in the SRE book's account, thousands of machines and disks failed in a typical year in one cluster \cite{sre-book}. Containers rely on Linux namespaces and control groups and share one kernel \cite{man7,kernel-docs,docs-docker-get-started-docker-concepts-the-basics-what-is-a-container}. In our reading, this links the job to process management, scheduling, virtual memory and resource control.

\paragraph{What we could not find.}
In the sources we read, we found no statement of which operating system Google SREs use on their own workstations. We found no total count of Google's business customers, only a 2023 figure for Google Workspace. The one SRE internship posting we found is limited to students in the EMEA region, and we found no SRE internship open to students in Thailand. We have no supported claim on web servers such as Apache or Nginx, or on running PostgreSQL, MongoDB or Redis. We also found no source on the specific projects given to student SREs beyond the general description in that posting. If none is open to us, we would look for SRE or DevOps internships elsewhere, which is an assumption. These gaps are noted where they arise. Anything about Google's internal environment that no source states is labeled as an assumption.

\paragraph{How GenAI can help in preparation and in the job.}
This is our recommendation and an assumption, drawn from how we used GenAI here. In preparation, GenAI can explain an unfamiliar command or log line and draft a README or postmortem outline for the lab. On the job, SRE teams write postmortems after incidents \cite{sre-book}, and GenAI can summarize a long log or timeline and draft the first version. It cannot confirm a root cause, because it does not see the real system, and our own checks found claims that went beyond their source. The SRE Workbook cautions that automation should not remove human understanding of what is going wrong \cite{workbook}. We therefore recommend GenAI for explanations, summaries and first drafts, with every statement checked against the logs, metrics and documentation. OS knowledge makes that check possible, because it lets a reader tell a correct explanation of processes, memory or sockets from a wrong one.

\paragraph{Reflection.}
GenAI sped up reading and organizing sources, but it made mistakes that human review had to catch. We kept Generative AI output as fact only where a source supported it, and we labeled the rest as recommendations or assumptions.
